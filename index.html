In 1804 the colony of New South Wales was a few farms along the rivers near Sydney and two small camps on the Derwent in Van Diemen’s Land. By the 1840s sheep and cattle stations reached deep into the Country of dozens of nations, from the Swan River to the Gwydir.

The land was not empty. Wherever the stations went, the people whose Country it was resisted, and colonists fought them. Governors wrote orders and proclamations. Soldiers, police and settlers went out. Some of what happened was written down. Much of it was not.

This file asks one question, again and again. What did the orders say, what happened, and did anyone answer for it?

How this works

Map the frontier, decade by decade. Compare what the official papers ordered with what the records say happened. Decode the words the records use. Then investigate four events. In each one, read what is agreed, ask three witnesses the same three questions, and write your finding.

What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed finding, if you can say why.

Every quotation is a real recorded word, from the source named under it. Every picture is a public-domain image or a silhouette. The See the source buttons open the original online.

Before you start

This file is about killings on the frontier. It names them and quotes the people who wrote about them. It does not show pictures of the dead. Some of the old documents use words that are offensive today. They are quoted as they were written, so that you can see how the writers thought. If anything here is upsetting, stop and talk to your teacher.

    Appin, 1816

    What the paper said

    Governor Macquarie · diary · 10 April 1816

    I therefore, tho, very unwillingly felt myself compelled, from a paramount Sense of Public Duty, to come to the painful resolution of chastising these hostile Tribes, and to inflict terrible and exemplary Punishments upon them without further loss of time … In the event of the Natives making the smallest show of resistance – or refusing to surrender when called upon so to do – the officers Commanding the Military Parties have been authorized to fire on them to compel them to surrender; hanging up on Trees the Bodies of such Natives as may be killed on such occasions, in order to strike the greater terror into the Survivors.

    Source. Lachlan Macquarie, “The Governor’s Diary & Memorandum Book”, 10 April 1816. Mitchell Library, State Library of New South Wales, A773; transcribed by the Macquarie Archive, Macquarie University.

    See the source (Macquarie Archive) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Governor Macquarie to Earl Bathurst · 8 June 1816

    … the Occurrence of most importance which took place was under Captain Wallis’s direction, who, having Surprized One of the Native Encampments and meeting with some resistance, killed 14 of them and made 5 Prisoners; among the killed there is every reason to believe that Two of the most ferocious and Sanguinary of the Natives were included …

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 9 (1917), pp. 139–140.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    Editor’s note · Historical Records of Australia · 1917

    The killed “included several women and children, who met their death by rushing in despair over precipices.”

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 9, note 36. The five prisoners were women and children.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    The order allowed firing if people resisted or refused to surrender. What does the record say happened, and to whom?

    Does the record match the order?

    Bathurst, 1824

    What the paper said

    Sir Thomas Brisbane · proclamation of martial law · 14 August 1824

    … being always mindful that the Shedding of Blood is only just, where all other Means of Defence or of Peace are exhausted; that Cruelty is never Lawful; and that, when personal Attacks become necessary, the helpless Women and Children are to be spared.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 11 (1917), pp. 410–411.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Sir Thomas Brisbane to Earl Bathurst · 3 November 1824

    … from private sources of information, I can state with some confidence that only seven Europeans have lost their lives in the conflict, which has taken place at Bathurst between the Natives and the Settlers. The number of the former, who have been killed, can only be gathered from conjecture, but in all probability they do not much exceed double the number of Europeans.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 11, p. 409. Other people at the time spoke of far more deaths. Nobody counted.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    The proclamation set limits. Could the governor know whether they were kept?

    Does the record match the order?

    Van Diemen’s Land, 1828 to 1832

    What the paper said

    George Arthur · proclamation of martial law · 1 November 1828

    … that the actual use of arms be in no case resorted to, if the Natives can by other means be induced or compelled to retire … that bloodshed be checked, as much as possible; that any Tribes which may surrender themselves up, shall be treated with every degree of humanity; and that defenceless women and children be invariably spared.

    Source. Hobart Town Courier, 8 November 1828, p. 1 (Trove).

    See the source (Trove) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What the records show · 1828 to 1834

    Roving parties of soldiers, police and convicts went out after the people inside the settled districts, and settlers went out on their own account. Colonists and Aboriginal people were killed in every district. In 1830 the Line of about 2,200 men caught two people and shot two others. Most of the surviving people were brought in by Robinson and taken to the Bass Strait islands.

    Source. Book Two, Part One, drawing on the House of Commons papers on Van Diemen’s Land (1831) and Lyndall Ryan, Tasmanian Aborigines (2012).

    The order said arms only as a last resort. What do the records suggest about how martial law worked in practice?

    Does the record match the order?

    Waterloo Creek, 1838

    What the paper said

    Lord Glenelg’s instruction, as Gipps understood it · 1838

    If any Aboriginal person died at the hands of the Queen’s officers, there was to be an inquiry.

    Source. Summary of Glenelg’s instruction, from Gipps to Glenelg, 25 April 1838, Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, pp. 396–400. These are not Glenelg’s own words.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Sir George Gipps to Lord Glenelg · 25 April 1838

    Your Lordship will observe that Major Nunn does not state in his report the number of men that were killed; but, from the conversation I have had with him, I should not think they were less than ten or twelve, besides a number wounded.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, p. 399. The inquiry was held at Invermein, more than 150 miles from the creek. No one was charged.

    Myall Creek, 1838

    What the paper said

    Lord Glenelg to Sir George Gipps · 16 November 1838

    No means occur to me more likely to check the natural desire for retaliation on the part of the natives than to impress them, if possible, with the conviction that the laws of the Colony will be equally administered for their protection from wrong and injury as for that of the European settlers … and an equal degree of punishment will be inflicted on the delinquent, whatever be his race or origin or colour.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, pp. 677–678.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    What the records say happened

    Sir George Gipps to Lord Glenelg · 19 December 1838

    The Report of the Judge (Mr. Justice Burton), who presided at the trial, was received by myself and the Executive Council on Friday, the 7th instt., when, no mitigating circumstances appearing in favor of any of them, and nothing to shew that any one of them was less guilty than the rest, the Council unanimously advised that the sentence of the law should take effect on them; they were accordingly ordered by me for Execution, and suffered yesterday morning at 9 o’clock.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, p. 703. Seven men were hanged on 18 December 1838. The other four were never tried again. The twelfth man, John Fleming, was never caught.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    Glenelg’s letter reached Sydney after the trials. Did the law treat the killing of Aboriginal people “equally” in this case? In every case?

    Port Phillip, 1835

    What the paper said

    Sir Richard Bourke · proclamation · 26 August 1835

    … every such treaty, bargain and contract with the Aboriginal Natives as aforesaid for the possession, title or claim to any Lands, lying and being within the limits of the Government of the Colony of New South Wales … is void and of no effect against the rights of the Crown; and that all Persons, who shall be found in possession of any such Lands as aforesaid, without the licence or authority of His Majesty’s Government for such purpose first had and obtained, will be considered as trespassers, and liable to be dealt with in like manner as other intruders upon the vacant lands of the Crown within the said Colony.

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 18 (1923), note 42, pp. 811–812.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What happened next · 1835 to 1837

    The proclamation did not send the settlers away. Their sheep and cattle stayed. In 1836 the government in Sydney accepted the settlement at Port Phillip, and in 1837 the town on the Yarra was named Melbourne.

    Source. Book Two, Part One; Bourke to Glenelg, 10 October 1835, Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 18, pp. 153–155.

    The proclamation called the settlers “trespassers”. What happened to them? Who was the land protected for?

    South Australia, 1836

    What the paper said

    King William IV · Letters Patent · 19 February 1836

    Provided Always that nothing in those our Letters Patent contained shall affect or be construed to affect the rights of any Aboriginal Natives of the said Province to the actual occupation or enjoyment in their own Persons or in the Persons of their Descendants of any Lands therein now actually occupied or enjoyed by such Natives.

    Source. Letters Patent establishing the Province of South Australia, 19 February 1836. Museum of Australian Democracy, Documenting Democracy, transcript.

    See the source (Documenting Democracy) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What happened next · 1836 onward

    On 28 December 1836 the province was proclaimed at Holdfast Bay, on Kaurna Country. Surveyors began laying out Adelaide in the new year. Land across the province was surveyed and sold.

    Source. Book Two, Part One; State Library of South Australia, “Proclamation of South Australia, 28 December 1836”.

    The proviso protected land “now actually occupied or enjoyed”. Who decided which land that was?

    The objects were two-fold — the civilization of the younger natives, and the creation of a force which would be seemingly better adapted, in the then existing circumstances of the colony, than any other to check, if not to prevent, the aggressions of the tribes upon the lives and property of the scattered European population.

    Words on the record

    The people who wrote the records chose their words. Some words hide more than they show. Tap each card to decode it.

    • An encounter or clash. Gipps wrote that a “rencontre had taken place” between the police and “a Tribe of the Natives”. He thought at least ten or twelve people were killed.

      Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, p. 397.

    • Asked what he meant by ordering his troopers to “disperse” a camp, Wheeler told the committee: “Firing at them.”

      Source. Select Committee on the Native Police Force (Queensland, 1861), as reprinted in Stone (1974).

    • A guess. Brisbane wrote that the number of Aboriginal people killed “can only be gathered from conjecture”, but that he could count the seven Europeans “with some confidence”.

      Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 11, p. 409.

    • Rule by the army. Under martial law, soldiers and magistrates could act without the ordinary courts.

      Source. Proclamations of 14 August 1824 and 1 November 1828.

    • The parts of the island where colonists had taken up land. Aboriginal people were ordered to keep out of them. Martial law covered them. The places left outside were the far south, Tasman’s Peninsula, the north-east and the west.

      Source. Hobart Town Courier, 8 November 1828.

    • What colonists called the line of about 2,200 men who walked across the settled districts in October and November 1830. Later it was called the Black Line.

      Source. Book Two, Part One; Lyndall Ryan (2012).

    • Land the government said belonged to the Crown and was not yet granted or sold. Bourke’s proclamation said settlers holding land under Batman’s deed would be treated like other “intruders upon the vacant lands of the Crown”.

      Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 18, note 42.

    • Colonists called it the Battle of Pinjarra. Binjareb Noongar people today call it the Pinjarra Massacre.

      Source. Book Two, Part One; Heritage Council of Western Australia.

    • The colonists called the Wiradjuri leader Saturday. His name is now usually written Windradyne.

      Source. Australian Dictionary of Biography, “Windradyne (1800–1829)”.

    • The island was named after Eliza Fraser’s shipwreck story. In 2023 its official name was changed back to K’gari, the Butchulla name.

      Source. Queensland Government (2023).

    • A Kulin ceremony by which visitors were given leave to pass through and use Country for a time. Some historians think the meeting with Batman in 1835 may have looked like tanderrum to the Kulin men. That is a later interpretation, not something written down in 1835.

      Source. Bain Attwood, Possession (2009); Museums Victoria.

    • The Gazette called the attack at Pinjarra “the punishment which had just been inflicted on them for their numerous murders and outrages”.

      Source. Perth Gazette, 1 November 1834, p. 382.

    Risdon Cove, 1804

    Was it an attack on the camp?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • In September 1803 Lieutenant John Bowen’s party of fewer than fifty people set up camp at Risdon Cove, on the eastern shore of the Derwent River.
    • In February 1804 David Collins arrived with a larger party and began a new camp across the river, which became Hobart Town. A small group stayed on at Risdon under Lieutenant William Moore.
    • On 3 May 1804 a large group of Aboriginal people came down the valley behind the Risdon camp. Soldiers fired on them. At the Hobart camp, the chaplain Robert Knopwood heard a cannon fired once from Risdon at two in the afternoon.
    • Aboriginal people were killed. The surgeon took in a little boy, about two years old, whose father and mother were both killed. Collins later ordered that the boy be returned to his own people.
    • Historians think the people in the valley were from the Oyster Bay or Big River nations, on a kangaroo hunt.
    • How many died at Risdon Cove has never been settled.

    Ask three witnesses

    Ask every witness the same three questions: What did they say they saw? Could they really have seen it? Why might they say it? Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    Jacob Mountgarret

    Surgeon at the Risdon camp · note to Robert Knopwood, written on 3 May 1804

    Source. James Backhouse Walker, “The English at the Derwent and the Risdon Settlement”, Papers and Proceedings of the Royal Society of Tasmania (1889), p. 83, from Knopwood’s diary for 3 May 1804.

    See the source (University of Tasmania ePrints) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “I beg to refer you to Mr. Moore for the particulars of an attack the natives made on the camp to-day, and I have every reason to think it was premeditated, as their number far exceeded any that we ever heard of. … The number of natives, I think, was not less than 5 or 6 hundred.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was at the Risdon camp that day, and wrote within hours. But he does not say what he saw himself. He leaves “the particulars” to Moore. His number, five or six hundred, is his own estimate.

    Why might they say it? He was writing to the chaplain on the other side of the river. Calling it an attack, and “premeditated”, explains why the soldiers fired. In the same note he invited the chaplain to christen the boy whose parents had been killed.

    Lieutenant William Moore

    Officer in command at Risdon · report to David Collins, 7 May 1804

    Source. House of Commons paper on military operations against the Aboriginal inhabitants of Van Diemen’s Land (1831), p. 53; reprinted in James Backhouse Walker, “The English at the Derwent and the Risdon Settlement”, Papers and Proceedings of the Royal Society of Tasmania (1889), pp. 83–84.

    See the source (University of Tasmania ePrints) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “It would appear from the numbers of them, and the spears, &c., with which they were armed, that their design was to attack us. However, it was not until they had thoroughly convinced us of their intentions, by using violence to a settler’s wife, and my own servant—who was returning into camp with some kangaroos, one of which they took from him—that they were fired upon. … During this time I was informed that a party of them was beating Birt, the settler, at his farm. I then despatched two soldiers to his assistance, with orders not to fire if they could avoid it.”

    Could they really have seen it? He commanded the soldiers and was at the camp. But part of his account is second-hand: “I was informed” that Birt was being beaten. He wrote four days later.

    Why might they say it? He was reporting to his commanding officer about why his soldiers had fired. He ended: “I have now, Sir, as near as I can recollect, given you the leading particulars, and hope there has nothing been done but what you approve of.”

    Edward White

    Convict who came out with Bowen · evidence to the Aborigines Committee, Hobart, 1830

    Source. James Backhouse Walker, “The English at the Derwent and the Risdon Settlement”, Papers and Proceedings of the Royal Society of Tasmania (1889), p. 85. Walker summarises White’s evidence. Only the words in quotation marks are White’s own.

    See the source (University of Tasmania ePrints) ↗

    What did they say they saw? He was hoeing ground near a farm up the valley, and says he was the first to see the people coming: about three hundred men, women and children, moving in a half-circle with kangaroos between them. They carried waddies, not spears. They “looked at him with all their eyes”. He said they did not threaten him, and “were not within half a quarter of a mile” of Birt’s hut. When the firing started, many were killed and wounded. He did not know how many.

    Could they really have seen it? He was in the valley, close to the people, before the firing. But he gave this evidence twenty-six years later, and we have it only as Walker summarised it in 1889.

    Why might they say it? In 1830 the colony was at war with the island’s people. Walker thought that “the witness had every inducement to represent their conduct in this affair in an unfavourable light”. Yet White’s account is the one least hostile to them.

    Why it still matters

    Risdon Cove is remembered as the first large killing of Aboriginal people in Van Diemen’s Land. In the next thirty years, the war on the island would take most of the people who lived there.

    Appin, 1816

    What happened at the camp near Broughton’s farm on 17 April 1816?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • Farms spread along the rivers west and south-west of Sydney: the Hawkesbury, the Nepean, the Cowpastures, Airds and Appin. This was the Country of the Dharug, the Dharawal and the Gundungurra.
    • In 1814 the Sydney Gazette reported killings at Appin, on both sides. They went on for two more years.
    • On 10 April 1816 Governor Macquarie sent out three detachments of the 46th Regiment, under Captain William Schaw, Captain James Wallis and Lieutenant Charles Dawe. Aboriginal men went with them as guides.
    • Early on 17 April 1816 Captain Wallis’s party came on a camp near William Broughton’s farm in the Appin district, on Dharawal Country. Fourteen people were killed. Five women and children were taken prisoner.
    • On 4 May 1816 Macquarie issued a proclamation explaining why he had sent the soldiers.

    Governor Macquarie’s orders · diary · 10 April 1816

    “I therefore, tho, very unwillingly felt myself compelled, from a paramount Sense of Public Duty, to come to the painful resolution of chastising these hostile Tribes, and to inflict terrible and exemplary Punishments upon them without further loss of time … In the event of the Natives making the smallest show of resistance – or refusing to surrender when called upon so to do – the officers Commanding the Military Parties have been authorized to fire on them to compel them to surrender; hanging up on Trees the Bodies of such Natives as may be killed on such occasions, in order to strike the greater terror into the Survivors.”

    Source. Lachlan Macquarie, diary, 10 April 1816. Mitchell Library, State Library of New South Wales, A773; Macquarie Archive transcription.

    See the source (Macquarie Archive) ↗

    Captain James Wallis

    46th Regiment · commanded the party · journal for 17 April 1816

    Source. Captain James Wallis, journal, 17 April 1816. State Archives of New South Wales, Colonial Secretary’s papers [4/1735, pp. 55–57]; transcript by Museums of History NSW. Spelling and punctuation as in the transcript.

    What did they say they saw? “before marching from quarters, I had ordered my men to make as many prisoners as possible, and to be careful in sparing, and saving the women and children my principal efforts were now directed to this purpose, I regret to say some had been shot, and others met their fate by rushing in despair over the precipice I was however partly successful, I led up two women and three children … fourteen dead bodies were counted in different directions.”

    Could they really have seen it? He commanded the soldiers and was there. But he also wrote: “it was moonlight the grey dawn of morn appearing, so dark as to be able only to discover their figures bounding from rock, to rock.”

    Why might they say it? He was writing the journal of an operation the governor had ordered, and he would have to account for it. He called it “a melancholy but necessary duty”.

    Governor Lachlan Macquarie

    Governor of New South Wales · despatch to Earl Bathurst in London, 8 June 1816

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 9 (Sydney, 1917), pp. 139–140.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “… the Occurrence of most importance which took place was under Captain Wallis’s direction, who, having Surprized One of the Native Encampments and meeting with some resistance, killed 14 of them and made 5 Prisoners; among the killed there is every reason to believe that Two of the most ferocious and Sanguinary of the Natives were included …”

    Could they really have seen it? He was not there. He was reporting what his officers told him, seven weeks later.

    Why might they say it? He was explaining to London an operation he had ordered himself. He does not say who the dead were.

    The editor of the Historical Records

    Editor’s note to Macquarie’s despatch · printed in 1917

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 9 (1917), note 36, pp. 854–855.

    What did they say they saw? The killed “included several women and children, who met their death by rushing in despair over precipices.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was not there. He was writing 101 years later, and the note does not say where his information came from.

    Why might they say it? An editor’s job is to explain the documents to readers. He added what Macquarie’s despatch left out.

    Why it still matters

    The killings at Appin were carried out by soldiers under a governor’s written orders. The orders, the officer’s journal and the governor’s report all survive. It is one of the best-recorded frontier killings.

    Investigation

    Myall Creek, 1838

    Why did Myall Creek end in a trial, when so many killings did not?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • Henry Dangar’s cattle station on Myall Creek was on Gamilaraay Country. In the autumn of 1838 a group of Wirrayaraay people, part of the Gamilaraay nation, were camped there. They had been invited by the stockmen. The hutkeeper, George Anderson, a convict, knew them well.
    • On Sunday 10 June 1838 twelve stockmen rode in. Eleven were convicts or former convicts. The twelfth, John Fleming, had been born in the colony.
    • Gipps reported that “not less than 28” people “of all ages and both sexes” were killed. Anderson refused to join in.
    • Governor Gipps sent a magistrate, Edward Denny Day, who arrested eleven of the men. Fleming was never caught.
    • On 15 November 1838 a jury found the eleven not guilty. Seven were tried again for the murder of a child, and found guilty on 29 November. They were hanged in Sydney on 18 December 1838. The other four were never tried again.
    • Earlier that year, at Waterloo Creek, mounted police had killed Gamilaraay people. No one was charged.

    George Anderson

    Convict hutkeeper at Myall Creek station · evidence at the second trial, November 1838

    Source. R. v. Kilmeister (No. 2), Supreme Court of New South Wales, 1838, from the newspaper report of the trial; Decisions of the Superior Courts of New South Wales, 1788–1899 (AustLII, [1838] NSWSupC 110).

    What did they say they saw? “I asked what they were going to do with the blacks, and Russel said, “We are going to take them over the back of the range, to frighten them”.”
    Of the girls and children who had been left behind, whom he sent away the next morning with ten Aboriginal men: “I did not like to keep them, as the men might come back and kill them.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was at the hut when the men came, and saw the people roped together and led away. He did not go with them.

    Why might they say it? Giving evidence against other convicts was dangerous. Museums of History NSW says convict witnesses were moved to the prison on Goat Island because of fears for their safety. He gave evidence at both trials.

    John Plunkett

    Attorney-General of New South Wales · opening speech for the prosecution, first trial, 15 November 1838

    Source. R. v. Kilmeister (No. 1), Supreme Court of New South Wales, 15 November 1838, from the Sydney Gazette, 20 November 1838; AustLII, [1838] NSWSupC 105. Spelling as printed.

    What did they say they saw? “The prisoners on being asked what they wee about to do with their captives said, and I believe it was Russsel who was spokesman, that they were going to take them to the mountains to frighten them.” And: “The information only shews at the utmost the death of two men, whereas, in fact, on the same day and in the same hour the lives of 28 individuals, men, women, and children, were sacrificed without any probable cause or provocation to palliate the atrocious crime in the sight of any laws human or divine.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was not there. He built his case from the evidence of Anderson, the superintendent William Hobbs and the magistrate Day. He says so himself: “I believe”.

    Why might they say it? His job was to persuade the jury. The charge named only one man, called Daddy, because his remains could be identified. Plunkett wanted the jury to know how many had really died.

    Sir George Gipps

    Governor of New South Wales · despatch to Lord Glenelg, 19 December 1838, the day after the hangings

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19 (Sydney, 1923), p. 701.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “On the afternoon of Sunday, the 10th June, a number of them suddenly surrounded the place, where more than thirty of the Blacks were assembled; they tied them all to a rope in the way that Convicts are sometimes tied, in order to be taken from place to place in the Colony, marched them to a convenient spot about a quarter of a mile off, and put them all, with the exception of one woman and four or five children, deliberately to death.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was not there. He relied on Day’s fifty-three-day investigation and the evidence at the trials.

    Why might they say it? He had to explain to London why seven colonists had been hanged for killing Aboriginal people, at a time when many colonists were angry about the trials.

    Why it still matters

    Myall Creek is one of very few frontier killings for which colonists were hanged. In 2000 a memorial was opened at the site. Descendants of the people who were killed and descendants of the men who killed them took part. In 2008 the site was added to the National Heritage List.

    Super Sleuths (optional): Waterloo Creek, and the witness who could not speak

    Sir George Gipps to Lord Glenelg · 25 April 1838

    “Your Lordship will perceive by the accompanying report, made to me by Major Nunn of the Mounted Police, that, previously to my arrival in the Colony, a rencontre had taken place between a part of the Police under his orders and a Tribe of the Natives, in which there is but too much reason to suppose that a number of the latter lost their lives.”
    “Your Lordship will observe that Major Nunn does not state in his report the number of men that were killed; but, from the conversation I have had with him, I should not think they were less than ten or twelve, besides a number wounded.”

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 19, pp. 397 and 399. “Rencontre” means an encounter or clash.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    Compare Waterloo Creek and Myall Creek. Who did the killing? Who investigated? Where was the inquiry held? What happened to the killers?

    Museums of History NSW says the only eyewitness to the killings at Myall Creek was an Aboriginal man, Davy, who worked at the station. His evidence could not be heard in court, because he was not a Christian and could not swear an oath.

    Whose evidence counted in a colonial court? What does that mean for the records of other killings?

    Paper and land, 1835 to 1836

    Did any of these papers recognise that the land belonged to the people already there?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • John Batman was a grazier from Van Diemen’s Land who had taken part in the roving parties of the Black War. In 1835 he and his partners wanted new grass for their sheep across Bass Strait.
    • In May 1835 he sailed into Port Phillip, the Country of the Kulin nations. With him were several Aboriginal men from New South Wales who could speak with the people of the bay.
    • On 6 June 1835 Batman met a group of Kulin men and came back with two deeds on parchment, with eight marks on them. He said they were the marks of eight chiefs.
    • He gave blankets, tomahawks, knives, scissors, looking-glasses, handkerchiefs, flour and shirts, and promised a yearly tribute.
    • On 26 August 1835 Governor Bourke declared every such treaty void. The settlers stayed. In 1837 the town on the Yarra was named Melbourne.
    • In February 1836 Letters Patent fixed the boundaries of the new province of South Australia. It was proclaimed on Kaurna Country on 28 December 1836, and its land was surveyed and sold.

    John Batman’s deed

    The larger deed, called Dutigalla · 6 June 1835

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 18 (Sydney, 1923), note 40, p. 811.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗

    What did they say they saw? It describes “… that Tract of Country, situate and being at Port Phillip, running from the branch of the River at the top of the Port, about 7 miles from the mouth of the River, Forty miles North East, and from thence West Forty miles across Iramoo Downs or Plains, and from thence South South West across Mount Vilanmarnartar to Geelong Harbour at the head of the same.”

    Could they really have seen it? Batman was there. But the deed is in English legal language, and the Kulin men did not read or speak English. Batman relied on the Aboriginal men from New South Wales to speak for him. Nobody now knows what the Kulin men understood was happening.

    Why might they say it? Batman and his partners wanted the land for their sheep. A deed signed by the “chiefs” might persuade the government that they had a right to it.

    Sir Richard Bourke

    Governor of New South Wales · proclamation, Sydney, 26 August 1835

    Source. Historical Records of Australia, series I, vol. 18, note 42, pp. 811–812.

    See the source (Internet Archive) ↗Portrait (Wikimedia Commons) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “… every such treaty, bargain and contract with the Aboriginal Natives as aforesaid for the possession, title or claim to any Lands, lying and being within the limits of the Government of the Colony of New South Wales … is void and of no effect against the rights of the Crown; and that all Persons, who shall be found in possession of any such Lands as aforesaid, without the licence or authority of His Majesty’s Government for such purpose first had and obtained, will be considered as trespassers, and liable to be dealt with in like manner as other intruders upon the vacant lands of the Crown within the said Colony.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was in Sydney, not Port Phillip. He was answering a question of law, not reporting what he had seen.

    Why might they say it? If private people could buy land from Aboriginal people, the Crown would lose control of who got the land, and of the money from selling it. His proclamation protected “the rights of the Crown”.

    The Letters Patent for South Australia

    Issued in the name of King William IV · London, 19 February 1836

    Source. Letters Patent establishing the Province of South Australia, 19 February 1836. Museum of Australian Democracy, Documenting Democracy, transcript.

    See the source (Documenting Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? Near the end, after the boundaries: “Provided Always that nothing in those our Letters Patent contained shall affect or be construed to affect the rights of any Aboriginal Natives of the said Province to the actual occupation or enjoyment in their own Persons or in the Persons of their Descendants of any Lands therein now actually occupied or enjoyed by such Natives.”

    Could they really have seen it? It was written in London by people who had never seen the land. It does not say who would decide which land was “actually occupied or enjoyed”.

    Why might they say it? The proviso shows that some people in London thought Aboriginal people had rights to land. It does not say how those rights would be protected.

    Did any of these papers recognise that the land belonged to the people already there?

    Why it still matters

    Batman’s deed is often described as the only documented attempt by colonists to make a treaty for land with Aboriginal people in Australia. The question it raised, who owned the land before the Crown, came back to the High Court more than 150 years later. That comes later in this series.

    Super Sleuths (optional): the missing witness

    There is no record of what the Kulin men said on 6 June 1835. All we have are eight marks, and what Batman wrote. Later historians suggest the meeting may have looked to the Kulin men like tanderrum, a ceremony by which visitors were given leave to pass through and use Country for a time. That is a later interpretation, not something written down in 1835.

    If the Kulin men thought they were welcoming visitors, and Batman thought he was buying land, was there an agreement at all? What would you need to know to decide?
